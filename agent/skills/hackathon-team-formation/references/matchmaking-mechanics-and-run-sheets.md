# Matchmaking mechanics: run sheets

Contents: the pitch-and-join round with its script and cut-off · the persistent board · the facilitated mixer · pre-event matching from registration data · no formal matchmaking · a positive and negative worked pair.

## 1. Pitch-and-join round - the default

The mechanic, from a civic-tech organizing guide: **"Hacking begins with project introductions. Participants that bring projects to the event have an opportunity to briefly (1 minute max) explain what they are working on at the very start of the event so that other participants can join that project."**

### Placement

Immediately after the welcoming session, not as a separate slot later. The source's day-of agenda runs the welcome first:

- introduce organizers
- thank venue and sponsors
- explain the event's history and purpose
- mention the code of conduct
- ask who has not been to a hackathon before
- explain logistics

Then: **"Anyone who has brought a project to work on should then introduce the project to everyone."** Hacking starts when the round ends.

One scale marker, easy to misread: **"In a small event (up to about 30 people), you can have all of the participants introduce themselves."** That threshold governs _everyone introducing themselves_, not the pitch round, which runs at any size.

### The pitch itself

The source specifies the content: **"Keep each pitch short: the leader's name and affiliation, a problem statement, the solution, and the skills/help needed."** The last item is what makes it a matchmaking mechanic rather than a showcase - a pitch that omits what help is wanted gives a listener nothing to join.

### Cut-off enforcement

This is the operational detail organizers most often skip: **"Project leaders tend to talk for as long as they can, so you may need to cut them off after one minute to be respectful of the audience's time."** Assign the cut-off to a named person before the round opens and say out loud that it will happen, so the first interruption is not read as rudeness.

The source also supplies the framing that makes pitchers comply: **"Encourage leaders to think of this not as recruiting but as boasting how awesome their day is going to be."**

### The rule that protects the round

**"Do not allow anyone to pitch an idea that they will not be working on at the event, unless there really are not enough ideas to go around. Otherwise, this is a waste of everyone's valuable time."** State it before the round, not after somebody has already done it. The exception in the second clause is real and worth keeping - at an event short of projects, an idea nobody will lead is still better than silence, provided everyone knows that is what it is.

### What the round produces

Groups described as "about 2-5 individuals" per project. That is an observed range at that author's events, not a target and not a rule. Your published size rule governs; this number only tells you what to expect.

### Sizing the round

Budget from the pitcher count, which Q3 and a registration field give you before the day. Nothing published states a maximum number of pitches or a point at which the round should be split into parallel rooms. **That is a gap, and any figure you set is your own.** The observable failure is a round that runs long enough for the audience to disengage before the last pitch, which is the one everyone joins least.

## 2. Persistent project board

A surface - physical or digital - where every project is written up and stays visible for the whole window, with what it is, who leads it and what help it needs.

The dominance check on the main menu rules it out: it reaches only whoever walks past it, it carries the project statement with nobody standing next to it to answer questions, and it still costs a surface, stocking and somebody keeping it current all day. It is kept on the page because it is what an organizer improvises when the pitch round gets cut for time - naming it is how you notice that happened.

It has one genuine use that is not matchmaking: as the **artifact of the pitch round**, written up as pitches happen, so a latecomer and the floor sweep both have somewhere to point. Run it as an output, not as a substitute.

## 3. Facilitated mixer

A structured people-first session - rotations, prompts, skill cards, a stated format - run before or instead of a pitch round, with a facilitator. There is no standard mixer format, so every detail of the one you run is yours to design.

Promotion condition, both halves required: a cohort mostly at its first hackathon, where too few people brought projects for the round to absorb the room, **and** a second space to run it in. A mixer in the hacking room displaces the hacking.

Its structural weakness is on the project-fit axis, and it is worth stating plainly to whoever asks for one. A mixer forms social groups that then have to invent a project, which is the slowest route to a first commit at an event whose whole constraint is time. Never run one without a stocked backlog behind it, so a group that gels has something to pick up instead of spending its first two hours in ideation.

## 4. Pre-event matching from registration data

Read the registrations before the day, propose groupings or route people to projects, and publish the result at or before check-in.

A civic-tech organizing guide's registration form asks:

- **"What kind of hacker are they?"** with the examples **"Developer. Designer. Data Scientist. Domain Expert. Government Staff. Communicator. Project Manager. Advocate."**
- "Are they new to hackathons?"
- "What are they interested in hacking on?"

It also instructs the organizer: **"Literally try to imagine how each registered participant will keep occupied at the event based on whatever information you know about them."** Separately, it runs a "Find project leaders" step ten days out, meeting leads to "Identify how they can take on newcomers, what tasks are doable for newcomers".

Reading the data and planning against it is established practice. Publishing an assigned team goes further.

Two hard constraints:

- **The field has to exist before registration opens.** Adding a role or interest question later reaches nobody who already signed up, which is usually the half you most need to place. Without the field the rung is not expensive, it is impossible.
- **Assignment and suggestion are different products.** A suggested grouping people may ignore costs the same reading time and none of the resentment. Prefer suggestion unless you have a reason on paper.

## 5. No formal matchmaking at all

A real rung. At an event whose cohort registers as formed teams, its coverage equals any other mechanic's for zero cost, and every minute spent on a mechanic is a minute taken from hacking.

Delete it the moment Q3 shows anyone registering without a team. From then on, silence is not a light touch, it is a decision about specific people.

## A worked pair

Both halves are this skill's own construction. Every figure in them is illustrative.

**Negative - a mixer that produced nothing.** A first-edition event, majority first-timers, no registration field on projects. The organizers ran a 45-minute icebreaker mixer in the hacking room, formed eleven groups by affinity, and started the clock.

By lunch, four groups had dissolved: they had gelled socially and then discovered nobody had a project, no backlog existed to pick from, and the two people who _had_ brought projects had been absorbed into groups that outvoted them. The mixer maximised coverage and starved project fit, which is exactly what the axis split predicts. _(Constructed composite illustrating the failure the source names - the third group, "participants struggling to find something relevant to work on" - not a report of a named event.)_

**Positive - the same cohort, reordered:**

1. Publish "Teams can be 1-4 people" with registration.
2. Ask on the form whether the registrant is bringing a project and what they want to work on.
3. Ten days out, meet the pitchers and get each project to the "ready-to-go tasks for newcomers" bar, with a build environment startable in under 20 minutes; stock four extra project ideas at the same bar.
4. Run the welcome, then the pitch round at one minute a pitch with a named cut-off enforcer.
5. Route everyone unplaced to the stocked list, owned by a named person.

The change cost twenty minutes of programme and a fortnight of lead time. The difference is entirely in what existed to join.

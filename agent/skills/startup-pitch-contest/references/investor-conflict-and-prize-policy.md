## Why the hackathon conflict shape is insufficient here

`samber/dev-event-organizer-skills@hackathon-judging`'s ladder answers interests in _who wins_: team closeness, employer and competing-organization ties, a sponsor funding a track. Removing the vote neutralises each one. An investor judge's interest is created by watching rather than by scoring, so it survives recusal; the skill body sets out all three ways it does.

The consequence for this page: the question is not only _who scores what_, but _what a judge may do afterwards with what they saw, and by when_. No rung of the hackathon ladder asks that, which is why the ladder below has a fourth rung the sibling's does not.

## The conflict ladder in detail

**No stated rule.** Nothing is asked and nothing is said, so the whole review lands after a judge has approached a company off a ranking they helped produce. Delete this rung as soon as a single active investor is on the panel.

**Scoring recusal only.** The hackathon shape, imported and honest about its limits. A judge abstains from scoring a company they are close to, or hold a competing position against, which is correct where the panel is operators and customers. Where the panel is investors, say what it does not cover: it removes the vote and leaves the access, and a founder who works that out afterwards will conclude the rule was decoration.

**Recusal plus disclosure to the pitching companies: the default.** Interests are collected in writing at invitation, and stated to the pitching companies before they pitch, not after the result.

- A conflict disclosed in advance is a term the applicant entered under.
- The same conflict disclosed after the announcement is a grievance.

Nothing about the facts changes between those two outcomes except the timing. When a room learns something matters as much as what the interest is.

Collect three things at invitation, in writing:

1. Does the judge or their institution hold a position in any applicant, or in anything competing with one?
2. Is the judge permitted by their own institution to sit on this panel and to see this material?
3. Does the judge intend to approach companies from this stage, and will they accept the contact protocol if one is published?

Question 3 is the one organizers skip, and it is the only one whose answer they can act on before the event rather than after it.

**Recusal plus disclosure plus a contact protocol: the starved rung.**

- Adds a stated stand-off window and a routed introduction path.
- It is the hardest rung to unwind, because it commits the organizer to police what independent professionals do with their own deal flow, and withdrawing it next edition reads as a retreat rather than a simplification.

Promote it when an award carries capital, or when a recurring contest's published rules already promise a process.

## Judge brief wording

Deliver in writing, before a judge sees a single application; not before scoring. Wording that works, at the right level of specificity:

> You will see companies here before most people do. That is part of why we invited you, and it is also why we ask three things. Tell us now if you or your fund hold a position in any applicant, or in anything competing with one; we will move you off that company's score. Tell us if your own firm's rules prevent you from sitting or from seeing this material; we would rather know now. And if you want to talk to a company after the block, tell us, and we will make the introduction. You are judging for the event today, not sourcing for your fund.

The last clause is the one doing the work, and it is deliberately an offer rather than a prohibition. A protocol that forbids contact will be ignored; one that routes it is easier to follow than to evade.

## The contact protocol, as publishable text

Only publish this when the starved rung was promoted. Four elements, published in the call so applicants read it before they apply:

1. **The stand-off**: judges do not approach pitching companies directly during a stated window after the block. State the window as a date, decided by the organizer. This skill states no length for it; the right answer is set by how long the result stays newsworthy at that event.
2. **The route**: a named organizer makes the introduction on request, from either side.
3. **What the founder controls**: a company may decline an introduction with no effect on its result, and the result is already final when the window opens.
4. **What is not covered**: a relationship that predates the contest, which is a declared interest rather than a new approach, and is handled by the disclosure above.

The protocol's purpose is not to prevent investment. It is to make sure the first conversation happens after the result rather than around it.

## The sponsor hard case

A signed agreement can give a sponsor a say in who pitches or who wins. The ladder cannot rank that away, and pretending otherwise produces advice the organizer cannot follow. Handle it the way `samber/dev-event-organizer-skills@hackathon-judging` handles a sponsor judging its own track:

- Confine the say to what was actually bought.
- Pair it with judges who have no tie to that sponsor.
- Compute the result with and without those scores.
- Disclose the composition before applications close.

One line is not negotiable: a stage slot in front of an investor audience is a speaking slot in every respect that matters, and it is not for sale, whatever else about the contest is. Per the DevOpsDays organizing guide: "no speaker spots can be bought by sponsors: not ever - period".

Where the clause is not signed yet, the drafting belongs to `samber/dev-event-organizer-skills@event-sponsor-agreement`, not here. Ask it for three things: the sponsor's say scoped to a named award rather than to the slate, a stated obligation to disclose the arrangement publicly, and no sponsor influence over who is accepted.

## The award commitment checklist

Run this per item, before anything is announced. An award that fails is bad; an award of capital that fails is a company that made decisions on it.

| Question                                                     | Why it is on the list                                                                                        |
| ------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Which named party delivers this item?                        | "The sponsor" and "the judges" are not parties who can be held to anything                                   |
| Is it in writing, from someone with authority to commit it?  | Enthusiasm in the room is real and is not a commitment                                                       |
| By what date does the winner receive it?                     | An open-ended award is an award nobody chases                                                                |
| What happens if the party does not deliver?                  | Decide this before, because after the event the organizer owns the gap personally                            |
| Does it carry capital, an instrument, or equity in any form? | If yes, the first gate fires and counsel sees the structure before the announcement                          |
| Does it require the winner to do anything to keep it?        | If yes, say in the call when the award becomes final; until then it is revocable and everyone should know    |
| Would the winner actually want it?                           | The award-fit dimension in the rubric exists for this; an award nobody uses is a delivery that never happens |

Three published contests show what "in writing, from someone with authority to commit it" looks like once the checklist reaches the actual payment:

- A major university business-plan competition releases no award until the recipient has filed the required tax documentation, and pays the company or its affiliated institution rather than an individual.
- A federal start-up challenge pays by electronic transfer subject to standard tax withholding and reporting, naming the specific approving official who signs off on the award.
- A recurring international pitch contest states plainly that its prize goes to the business named in the application, not to whichever person happens to be standing on stage.

None of the three leaves the payment mechanism to be worked out after the announcement, and none of this transfers a currency amount into this skill.

## The routing ladder for the three gates

**This skill states no numeric threshold, no exemption name, no form name and no currency amount.** A plausible-sounding threshold in a document telling an organizer how to structure an award to a company is worse than no guidance at all: an award of this kind plausibly intersects employment or securities rules in some jurisdictions.

Name the live categories and route each one:

- **Capital, a convertible instrument, or equity awarded as a prize**: route to counsel before the award is announced, and to the funding party's own legal team where one exists. Jurisdiction-specific, and it changes.
- **Contest-law classification**: whether the competition counts as skill-based or as something else where it runs, which changes the regime that applies. This is why the tie-break ladder refuses a random draw, and why that refusal is stated as a routing rather than as a rule.
- **The judge's own institutional obligations**: not the organizer's to interpret. Route to the judge, in writing, before confirming the invitation.
- **Custody of application material**: what was collected, who read it, how long it is kept. Route to whoever holds the organization's data obligations; where nobody does, that absence is itself the finding to report.
- **Recording, streaming and press in the room**: route to the applicants themselves, before they apply. Consent is the only mechanism here, and it cannot be retrofitted.

Route each live category. Never answer one.

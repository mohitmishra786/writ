## The published route, written as a sentence

Write this before building any form, and publish it verbatim. It is the artifact a founder reads to decide whether to spend an evening applying.

The sentence names four things and nothing else: **who may apply**, **how the slate is chosen**, **who chooses it**, and **what an accepted company is committing to**. Example shape:

> Any company at [stage, described] may apply by [date]. Applications are read by [named reviewers or roles] against the published criteria below; [number of rounds] round(s). Accepted companies commit to [pitching in person on the date] and to [anything else - a rehearsal, a photo, a recording].

If the sentence cannot be written without a hedge, the funnel rung is not decided yet. A hedge in this sentence becomes a dispute on the day.

## The application form, field by field

Ordered by what the review actually uses. Each field exists because a criterion needs it, and a field no criterion reads should be cut - the reason is the custody section below.

| Field                                                 | What the review uses it for                                                                                                        |
| ----------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Company name and one-line description                 | Deduplication, and the first legibility check: a company that cannot say what it does in one line will not do it better on a stage |
| Stage, in the call's own words                        | Eligibility against Q2's population, and the only fair comparison basis across the slate                                           |
| What the company does, in prose                       | The substance of the topic score                                                                                                   |
| Who is on the team and what they did before           | The founder dimension, and the only source for it - the stage shows presence, not history                                          |
| Traction, stated as facts with dates                  | The claimed dimensions. This is the field that makes verification possible at all                                                  |
| What the company wants out of pitching                | The single best predictor of whether the award on offer fits the applicant                                                         |
| Who is presenting, and are they available on the date | The travel and availability gate - asked here, not after acceptance                                                                |
| Any conflict the applicant already knows about        | An applicant frequently knows a judge's fund is an investor or a competitor before you do                                          |
| Consent to the recording and press posture            | Only meaningful if it names the actual posture, which is why it is asked here and not assumed                                      |

Two fields to leave out unless a criterion genuinely reads them: a full deck, and any financial detail beyond the traction facts. Both are custody-bearing, neither is scoreable in the time a reviewer has, and asking signals a diligence process the contest is not running.

Published applications confirm this shape while going considerably further, which is a reason for restraint rather than a reason to copy them. A recurring international pitch contest's own application collects company stage, monthly and annualized revenue, revenue growth rate, last funding round and lead investors, target market size, and each founder's equity share, alongside narrative fields matching the problem, product and team rows above. A major university business-plan competition asks for a similarly structured executive summary: company summary, founder backgrounds, customer and market analysis, competitive differentiation, and the amount of investment sought.

Both confirm the table's shape; neither is a reason to pull in their full field list. Every field collected past a criterion that reads it is custody taken on for nothing.

The what-it-does and what-it-wants fields separate two dimensions: positioning and award fit. A company's "why me" asks whether the award on offer is the one it needs, unlike an individual speaker's authority on a subject, which shapes the selection of a talk. Score positioning and award fit apart.

## Which fields are custody-bearing

A custody-bearing field is one whose content the applicant would not publish. Every one of them creates an obligation the contest has to state and cannot walk back once a reviewer has read it.

- Traction facts, any financial detail, a deck, and anything said on a screening call.
- The rule that follows: **name, in the call, who reads applications and what happens to them afterwards.** "The organizing team" is not a name. If reviewers include people who invest, say so - that is the same fact the conflict rule exists to handle, met earlier and more cheaply.
- Delete, do not archive, what the review no longer needs. An application store that outlives the contest is a store nobody is minding.
- Pick the deletion trigger from your own jurisdiction's claims-limitation period, not from a round number that feels safe. A recurring international pitch contest's own published privacy notice sets its deletion date to a stated number of years after the award is completed, reasoned explicitly from the statutory period during which a claim connected to the contest could still be brought. Copy the reasoning, not the figure: the right window is set by where the contest runs, not by this one.

## Criteria in the call itself

Publish the criteria and their weights in the call, before applications close. The reason carries over unchanged from a sibling skill: "Undisclosed weights score a competition nobody entered" (`samber/dev-event-organizer-skills@hackathon-judging`).

Stage (Q2) is rarely the only eligibility gate in force. Check each of these before publishing the template's point 1 below, and name only what applies:

- A founding-date cap, so a mature company does not crowd out the population the contest exists for.
- A vertical or technology-fit requirement, when the contest sits inside an event built around one.
- A business-model filter separating a product company from a services or agency business - the two compete on different criteria and rarely belong on the same stage.
- A geographic-anchoring requirement, when the contest ties to a region's own economic-development goals rather than to the technology alone.

An axis that exists only in the reviewers' heads is one an applicant cannot self-select against.

The template shape: five lines, published as they will be applied:

1. Who is eligible, in the stage vocabulary the call already used.
2. Each criterion, named, with its integer weight.
3. Which criteria are checked against the application and which are judged on the stage. Founders optimise for the second and neglect the first unless told.
4. Whether a screening round exists, and what it decides.
5. What the award is, and which party delivers each item.

Point 5 belongs in the call rather than in a later announcement because it is the term the applicant is actually deciding on.

## The screening call, and what it cannot do

Only run this when the funnel menu's starved rung was promoted. It is a short live conversation with a shortlisted applicant, before the slate is fixed.

Screening calls before a slate is fixed are real, published practice, not a construction here. A federal start-up challenge runs a staged, scored review that funnels its strongest submissions into a screening call with programme staff before finalists are recommended for approval. A recurring international pitch contest's own published rules reserve the right to request "an interview to discuss your application" from any applicant before the jury finalizes who advances.

Neither publishes what the call itself has to cover beyond feeding a later decision, which is why the three rules below exist.

What it is for: **testing whether the founder can hold a room**, which is the one thing the form cannot show and the one thing the stage block will spend its minutes on.

Three rules that keep it defensible:

- Ask every shortlisted applicant the same opening question, and say in the call that a screening round exists. A conversation some applicants got and others did not is the most contestable thing in the funnel.
- Take notes against the published criteria only. A note that does not map to a criterion is a note that will decide the slate without appearing in the reasons.
- It is not diligence. It does not verify traction, it does not review documents, and it must not be presented to the applicant as either. The moment it looks like diligence, the applicant discloses on that basis.

What it cannot do: fix a slate that outreach never reached, and rescue an applicant who applied to the wrong contest. Both are funnel-entry problems, and neither is repairable at the screening stage.

## Timeline anchors

Structure the timeline in four anchors, in order: call opens, call closes, slate notified, stage.

The one hard floor sits between notification and the stage: "People will usually need at least 4-6 weeks to arrange for travel or time off" (DevOpsDays organizing guide). The figure was measured on individual speakers, and it transfers here as a floor rather than a fit: an accepted startup may need more than one person away from a business they are running.

Two recurring contests publish the anchor gaps themselves, useful as a range rather than a target:

- TechCrunch's Startup Battlefield: about ten weeks from application deadline to the public announcement of its selected cohort, then about eight weeks from that announcement to the stage.
- SXSW Pitch: roughly four months from its final application deadline to the stage.

Both ranges sit well clear of the DevOpsDays floor.

Below that floor, the open rungs of the funnel menu stop working and the slate goes to invitation - the same conclusion, for the same reason, that the CFP sibling reaches when its own floor is breached.

## A positive and a negative published route

**Negative - a route that reads as open and is not:**

> We're looking for exciting startups to pitch at the event! Reach out if you're interested and we'll be in touch.

Nothing here can be applied to. It names no date, no criteria, no reviewer and no award, so the slate will be assembled from whoever the organizers already know: the curated rung, run without saying so. An applicant who is not already in the network cannot tell that they have already lost.

**Positive - the same contest, decided**

> Companies with a live product and their first paying customers may apply until [date]. Applications are read by [named reviewers] against the published criteria and weights below, in a single review pass. Nobody else reads them, and they are deleted by [date]. Accepted companies are notified by [date] and commit to pitching in person on [date]. The winner receives introductions to [named investors, listed], made by [named organizer] by [date]; no capital is awarded.

Every sentence is checkable afterwards, which is the whole point: the route, the readers, the retention, the dates, and the award with its deliverer.
